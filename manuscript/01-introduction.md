# 1. Introduction

Somewhere near the front of most national curricula there is a page that does not look like the rest of the document. It has no outcomes on it, no content maps, no assessment guidance. It lists perhaps ten qualities and says that this is the kind of person the system exists to produce. Ministries print it on posters. Head teachers read it out in September. It is the part of a curriculum that people who will never open the curriculum can still recite.

Learner profiles carry a heavy load for their length, and they carry it in public. Everything a system is prepared to say about the person it wants has to fit on that page, and everything it is not prepared to say has to be kept off it. That makes the profile an unusually direct object of study. Apple's (2004) argument that curricula encode decisions about whose knowledge and whose identities count usually requires excavation; in a learner profile the decisions are already on the surface.

This article compares two of them. The International Baccalaureate learner profile is the reference case in the genre, ten attributes running across all four IB programmes since the late 1990s and revised most recently in 2017. The Türkiye Yüzyılı Maarif Modeli — the Türkiye Century Education Model, hereafter the Maarif Model — is new. Approved by Türkiye's Board of Education in May 2024 and in force from September of that year, it replaced the architecture that had governed Turkish schooling for a generation, and it did so around a stated end: raising *yetkin ve erdemli insanlar*, competent and virtuous people.

The two profiles are the same length and the same shape. Each names ten qualities. Each elaborates them in short passages. Each refuses to separate what a graduate should know from what a graduate should be. Set the lists beside one another and the obvious study writes itself: match the items, note that Türkiye adds patriotism and aesthetics where the IB adds open-mindedness and reflection, conclude that a national system emphasises belonging where a transnational one emphasises mobility.

We did that study first, and it was true and slight. The overlap between the two sets is large enough that a comparison of contents mostly documents the conventions of the genre. What the genre pushes every system towards, inquiry and integrity and care and courage, turns out to be what both systems say. The differences that survive that filter are real but modest, and they do not explain why the two documents feel so unalike to read.

The question we ended up with came from elsewhere. Discourse-analytic work on curriculum policy has long held that policy texts do not merely describe students but make positions available to them: identity resources that a reader may take up, be assigned, or fail to fit (Weedon, 1997; Søreide, 2007). Hilt, Riese and Søreide (2018) used this to show how a Norwegian reform document assembled an ideal student who was creative, responsible and above all self-regulated, and then asked the question that gives such analysis its point: who will not be recognised as an ideal student here? Şen (2020), analysing Turkish education reform discourse, tracked the shifting use of *we* and *our* to establish how the text placed itself in relation to those it addressed.

Read that way, the two profiles stop looking alike almost immediately. So the questions we pursue are these:

What position does each profile make available to the student? How is that position produced in the text? And who could not take it up?

We answer them by reading both documents closely in their original languages, attending to how each addresses its reader, whether it names the collective the student is answerable to, and what conditions it attaches to the freedoms it grants. Section 2 sets out the two documents and the very different situations they were written in, including the Turkish reform context, which is not well known outside Türkiye and which bears directly on how the Maarif Model reads. Section 3 describes our approach. Section 4 reports what we found. Section 5 considers what follows for how learner profiles are read and written.
