# 2. Two profiles, two situations

Learner profiles are written by institutions with problems to solve, and the problems are not the same. Before comparing the texts it is worth setting out what each was written to do.

## 2.1 A profile that has to travel

The IB learner profile serves schools that have almost nothing else in common. They sit in different countries under different ministries, teach different populations in different languages, and answer to different regulators. What they share is the IB, and the profile is a large part of what the sharing consists of: Wells (2011) describes it as the thread running through the four programmes and tying them together.

A document in that position cannot be very specific. Whatever it says has to be sayable in Jakarta and Geneva and Nairobi, and has to survive being read by teachers whose own formation was national. Generality is not a failure of nerve here; it is the condition of the profile working at all.

It is also, on the evidence, expensive. Tocci, Ryan and Ensminger (2021) followed IB humanities teachers across a single school district and found them confident that they were teaching the profile and implementing it in materially different ways, with the variation traceable to the breadth of the concept underneath. Metlí and Lane (2020) report the same instability in international-mindedness itself, the construct the profile exists to express, which after decades of use still resists a settled definition. Wells (2011), examining the profile directly, noted that it asserts values at length while saying almost nothing about how values are acquired — a gap that matters more, not less, when the document is the main thing holding a federation together.

The critical literature has read this institutionally rather than pedagogically. Bunnell (2010) analyses the IB as a system serving a mobile and comparatively privileged constituency, and Resnik (2012) situates it within a sociology of international education in which such systems supply credentials that cross borders. Hill (2012) supplies the longer view, tracing international-mindedness back through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. None of this treats the profile as a neutral statement of educational good, and neither do we.

## 2.2 A profile that has to settle something

The Maarif Model was written for a system with one ministry, one language of instruction, and a curriculum history in which the aims of schooling have been repeatedly and publicly contested.

Turkish curriculum policy has moved in large steps. A constructivist reform in 2005–2006 reorganised the national curriculum around student-centred learning; scholars judged it under-prepared and poorly supported, and found that classroom practice changed less than the documents did (Ersözlü, Karakuş & Karakaş, 2022). Further revisions followed in 2017–2018. Across the same period the ideological register of Turkish education policy became a subject of sustained scholarly attention. Şen (2020), analysing a foundational reform report, conceptualises the governing political ideology as Islamic nationalism and argues that the report's discourse recasts citizenship education, describes Türkiye's population as homogeneous, and omits the vocabulary of democracy and human rights. Whether or not readers accept that characterisation, which is one scholar's conceptualisation, advanced in a critical tradition and contested inside Türkiye, the underlying observation is not in dispute: in Türkiye, what schools are for is an openly political question, and curriculum documents are read as answers to it.

The Maarif Model entered that setting. The Board of Education approved it on 23 May 2024 and it came into force on 9 September, phased in through pre-school, the first year of primary, the fifth year of middle and the ninth year of upper secondary schooling. The ministry reported consulting more than sixty-seven thousand stakeholders during its development.

Two features of the document announce its ambitions before any analysis begins.

The first is its name. Turkish has a serviceable modern word for education, *eğitim*, and the model does not use it in its title. *Maarif* is an Ottoman-era term, from an Arabic root associated with knowing and recognition, and it carries a semantic history in which instruction, moral formation and civilisational inheritance are not separate activities. It was the name of the ministry itself until 1935 and again is not. Choosing it signals that the framework means to be understood as continuous with something, and the document says as much: its opening section is headed *Köklerden Geleceğe*, from the roots to the future.

The second is the stated end. The model does not open with skills or standards but with a person: "Türkiye Yüzyılı Maarif Modeli'nin nihai hedefi, yetkin ve erdemli insanlar yetiştirmektir" — the ultimate aim of the model is to raise competent and virtuous people. *Yetkinlik* covers the knowledge and skills a field requires. *Erdem* is glossed in the text as *ruhsal olgunluk*, spiritual or inward maturity, "başka bir ifadeyle ahlaken övülen meziyetler", in other words qualities that are morally praised. The two are presented as completing one another rather than as separate goals.

Underneath the profile sit four constructs that the document supplies about itself. *Ontolojik bütünlük* treats the person as *ruh* and *beden*, spirit and body, and defines *ruh* as the sum of intellectual, moral and emotional faculties. *Epistemolojik bütünlük* runs from knowledge to *bilgelik*, wisdom, which arrives when "bilgi ve erdem" come together. *Zamansal bütünlük* extends the learner's relevant past beyond their own biography to "milletçe sahip olunan ortak bilinç, kültür ve değerler", the shared consciousness, culture and values held as a nation. *Aksiyolojik olgunluk*, axiological maturity, is named as the unifying dimension and the foundation of the values framework. We return to these in Section 3, because they do analytic work for us as well as for the ministry.

## 2.3 What has been written about the Maarif Model

Very little, in English. The framework is two years old, and the scholarship that exists is Turkish and has moved in a particular direction. Kadıoğlu Ateş (2024) places the competent and virtuous person in a lineage of moral philosophy running from the Sophists through Locke and Dewey to Kohlberg. Bayrak (2025) sets the ten characteristics against the *Kutadgu Bilig*, the eleventh-century Turkish work of statecraft and ethics, and finds substantial correspondence along with a clear difference: the older text counsels rulers and grounds its counsel religiously, while the Maarif Model addresses every student and frames development in contemporary terms. Karaoğulları (2026) turns the ten characteristics into a coding instrument and applies them to a children's novel.

These are useful studies and they share an orientation. Each establishes what the profile inherits. None sets it against a framework from outside the tradition it inherits from, and so none has had occasion to ask what the profile does to the student who reads it.

That is the gap this article addresses, and there is a reason to approach it carefully. Takayama, Sriprakash and Connell (2017) describe a habit in comparative and international education by which a non-Western case becomes intelligible only as a departure from an unmarked Western norm, with the analytic categories arriving already carrying the verdict. Coding the Maarif Model through the IB profile's vocabulary would do exactly that. Manzon (2011) puts the methodological version of the point: units of comparison in this field are constructed, and the construction is a claim to be defended rather than a preliminary to be assumed. Both warnings shaped how we proceeded.
