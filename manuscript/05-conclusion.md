# 5. Concluding discussion

## 5.1 Where the genre converges and where it does not

The contents of these two profiles are more alike than their reputations suggest. Both want students who inquire, who think critically, who act with integrity, who care for others and who are willing to be brave. A study that compared only what the two documents list would have found a large shared core and a modest fringe of local additions, and would have concluded that a national system and a transnational one converge on the educated person while differing at the edges.

That conclusion is available from the evidence and we think it is the wrong one, because the shared core is largely an artefact of the genre. A profile has to fit on a page and be repeatable by people who have not read the curriculum; the qualities that survive those constraints are the ones no constituency will object to. Convergence at the level of the list tells us mostly about what learner profiles can say.

What the two documents do not share is how they say it, and that turns out to carry the argument. One addresses the student and the other describes them. One names the collective the student serves and the other leaves it indefinite. One returns the student to themselves after every encounter and the other does not. None of this appears in an inventory of values, and all of it shapes the place the student is offered.

We take this to be the main methodological implication. Research on learner profiles has concentrated on their contents, and their contents are the part of the genre least likely to distinguish anything.

## 5.2 Why the two positions cannot be blended

Comparative work on national and transnational frameworks tends to arrive at a recommendation: take the moral depth of the one and the openness of the other, and build something that has both. We do not think that is available, and the reason is structural rather than political.

The Maarif Model can specify what a good person consists of, at the level of sixty-one named components, because it knows who it is speaking to. Specification of that kind requires a destination. The IB cannot specify at that grain, because its profile must be legible in schools that share nothing but the IB, and Tocci, Ryan and Ensminger (2021) show what its generality already costs in classrooms even at the level it operates. The two levels of specification are not virtues and vices; each is what its institutional situation permits.

The same logic runs through the clause of return. A profile can name the tradition its student must preserve, or it can leave the outcome of encounter genuinely open. It cannot do both, because naming what must be preserved is what forecloses the outcome. A framework that named a collective and then declined to protect it would be making a promise it had no way to keep. Recommending that systems combine the two mistakes a trade-off for an oversight.

What can be transferred is smaller and more useful than a hybrid. Each profile could learn what the other's form makes visible. A profile that names its collective can be argued with by the people it names; a profile that does not name one is protected from that argument, and the protection is not obviously a good thing. Conversely, a profile that attaches no conditions to openness has not thereby avoided the question of what it wants preserved — it has only left the answer implicit, which makes it harder to examine.

## 5.3 What this means for writing a profile

The choices that did most work in our analysis are choices drafting groups rarely deliberate over. Whether to write *we* or *the student*. Whether to name what the student is answerable to or to leave it general. Whether to qualify a grant of independence. These are usually settled by the house style of the ministry or the habit of the drafter, and they determine how the document positions its reader more decisively than the selection of qualities does, which is where drafting committees spend nearly all of their time.

The exclusion analysis in §4.5 is the practical payoff, and it generalises beyond these two texts. Any profile can be read for who is unable to occupy the position it offers, and the reading is quick: find the point where the document assumes something about the student's starting commitments, and ask which students do not start there. For the Maarif Model that point is a subordinate clause about what independent thought must stay consistent with. For the IB it is a requirement to hold one's inheritance open, and a demand for a second language. Neither system, on the evidence of its profile, appears to have asked the question.

## 5.4 Limits

We have analysed documents, and only documents. What Turkish and IB teachers do with these texts is a separate question requiring separate methods, and nothing here should be read as evidence about classrooms. Ball's (1994) distinction between policy as text and policy as discourse marks the boundary: we reach the first and touch the edge of the second.

The comparison is asymmetrical in ways that can be declared but not corrected. One profile governs a system and the other moves between them. One was read in a first language and the other in a second. One has three decades of interpretive apparatus around it and the other has been in force for two years, which means our reading of the Maarif Model has less scholarship to be checked against than our reading of the IB.

The clause of return rests on three instances. That is enough to establish that the construction is used and to show what it does; it is not enough to establish a rate, and we make no claim about frequency. The Maarif Model is also recent and under revision, and our findings are indexed to the 2025 edition of the *Ortak Metni*.

Finally, the ten-against-ten symmetry that made this comparison tractable belongs to how the documents present themselves. It licenses comparison of structural position, and Table 1 accordingly reports adjacencies rather than equivalences.

## 5.5 Ending

The two documents we have read are trying to do something difficult in very little space: say what a person is for, in a way that a teacher can repeat and a fourteen-year-old can hear. Both do it seriously. Neither is the cynical exercise that critical curriculum scholarship sometimes expects to find, and neither is innocent either.

What separates them is not the depth of their moral ambition, which is comparable, nor the range of their view, which is easy to mistake for a difference in generosity. It is what each does with the student's inheritance. The Maarif Model treats it as the thing being handed on, and builds a person who can be trusted with it. The IB treats it as one of the things to be examined, and builds a person who can survive the examination. Each produces someone the other has no place for.

That is worth stating plainly, because the field's habitual move at this point is to hope for a synthesis, and the hope obscures what is actually at stake. These are not two dialects of a shared educational language. They are two answers to the question of whether a person should be able to come home.
